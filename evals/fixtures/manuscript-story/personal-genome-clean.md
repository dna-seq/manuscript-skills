# **Just-DNA-Lite: a Local-First, Open-Source Platform for Personal Genome Annotation and Polygenic Risk Scoring**

*Excerpt: Sections 2 and 4, most of Sections 3 and 5, the figures, the references and the supplement are not included.*

## **Abstract**

Just-DNA-Lite is the core of an open-source ecosystem for reading a personal genome against published evidence on the user's own computer. Its web platform annotates a whole-genome VCF and computes polygenic risk scores locally, so the genome does not have to be uploaded. Every matched variant links to its reported effect and its sources. What the platform reports comes from modules, versioned tables of variant-trait associations that researchers write without programming. Ready-made modules cover longevity, disease predisposition and drug response. Researchers publish new ones to just-dna-registry, an open registry that works like an app store, where others browse, install and apply them with no software update. The just-module-creator plugin drafts modules from the literature and public databases inside the AI assistant a researcher already uses. Polygenic scores come from just-prs, a library that also works on its own. It places each PGS Catalog score that passes its quality audit within a reference population matched to the genome's inferred ancestry and shows all models for a trait side by side, so users see where the models agree and differ. AI assistants can also run annotation and scoring through Model Context Protocol servers, and a public demonstration lets anyone try the platform on public genomes.

## **1. Introduction**

Researchers and individuals who receive raw variant files (VCFs) containing millions of variants
can find it hard to annotate them against published databases without bioinformatics and programming expertise (Chen et al., 2023), and acquiring that expertise takes time away from the questions they set out to answer. Many consumers who download their raw data from a testing company take it to third-party interpretation tools (Nelson et al., 2019; O'Daniel et al., 2025).

By 2025, manufacturers advertised the sequencing of a human genome for US$80 to US$200 (Illumina, 2023; Ultima Genomics, 2025). Clinical laboratory studies report higher costs after including labour, computing and analysis, with estimates depending on the workflow and costing scope (Mordaunt et al., 2024; Ehman et al., 2025). The National Human Genome Research Institute (NHGRI) estimates that the first human genome cost US$500 million to US$1 billion (NHGRI, 2021). Several companies now offer direct-to-consumer sequencing services (O'Daniel et al., 2025).

Existing solutions generally belong to two extremes. Commercial platforms such as DNA Complete (formerly Nebula Genomics) and Dante Labs provide curated reports, but the curation is opaque: users cannot verify which polymorphisms contributed to a score, whether the methodology is appropriate for their ancestry, or how findings were weighted. At the other end, research-grade tools like PLINK (Chang et al., 2015), ANNOVAR (Wang et al., 2010) and VEP (McLaren et al., 2016) are powerful but demand command-line proficiency and a lengthy setup. Open-source projects between these extremes each cover part of the task, such as local annotation with installable modules, ancestry-normalized scoring of PGS Catalog models, or access to a genome from an AI assistant. Table S7 compares the documented features of 23 tools for personal-genome annotation, polygenic scoring and AI-assisted interpretation; none of them documents a graphical interface, annotation sets added without code, PGS Catalog scoring and ancestry-matched percentiles together.

Our own starting point was longevity research: the first generation of this platform was built to read a personal genome against studies of longevity (Kulaga et al., 2024), and longevity remains its most extensive expert-curated module (Section 3.1, Table 1). Recent progress in the genetics of longevity also shows why an annotation tool has to be extensible. After correction for extrinsic mortality, the intrinsic heritability of lifespan has been estimated at about 50%, against 20–25% in twin studies (Shenhar et al., 2026). Genetic association studies have repeatedly linked loci such as APOE and FOXO3 to longevity (Broer et al., 2015; Caruso et al., 2022). Databases such as LongevityMap within the Human Ageing Genomic Resources (HAGR) (Tacutu et al., 2018) have compiled thousands of variants tested for association with longevity, but we found no tool that makes it straightforward to annotate a personal VCF against these databases transparently and extensibly. More broadly, variant-trait associations are published faster than any single curation team can integrate them, which supports the case for platforms where new annotation modules can be added rapidly.

Such a platform has to be easy to use, transparent in its methods and extensible without code. It also has to protect privacy. Genomic data are among the most sensitive personal information, and uploading them to third-party servers carries risks that individuals and regulations such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) seek to avoid.

Just-DNA-Lite is a local, open-source platform for annotating a personal genome and computing polygenic scores. We developed it as a ground-up rewrite of Just-DNA-Seq (Kulaga et al., 2024), whose first generation (Generation I) was a set of annotation and report modules for OakVar, a Python variant-analysis framework derived from OpenCRAVAT (Pagel et al., 2020). Just-DNA-Lite (Generation II) removes the OakVar dependency entirely and uses a standalone annotation engine. It also replaces Python annotation plugins with declarative modules discoverable from configured sources (Section 2.2). Supplementary S1 describes Generation I.

The resulting workflow lets users choose published annotation content, apply it to their own genome and inspect the evidence behind the report. The reusable just-prs scoring library adds polygenic scores and reference-population comparisons to the same web interface. Researchers can contribute new annotation modules through the open-source registry, and Model Context Protocol (MCP) interfaces make the annotation and scoring operations available to AI assistants. People with different needs enter this workflow at different points: the main features are listed below, and Box 1 names the user groups and where each starts.

**Key features.**

- **A cleaned PGS Catalog, ready to use.** just-prs mirrors the PGS Catalog's harmonized scoring files as Parquet, with cleaned metadata in which genome-build labels are normalized and published performance metrics are parsed into numbers. Its reference distributions cover 6,117 scores for 778 traits in five 1000 Genomes superpopulations. Each distribution is audited before release; 860 scores were withheld, 847 of them because most of seven public test genomes fell in the outermost 1% of the reference distribution with scores off its scale, which shows that a real genome's score would not be on the reference scale. Each result gives its percentile in the reference population matched to the genome's inferred ancestry, its coverage and a quality label (Section 3.2).
- **An app store for annotation modules.** Modules are data: versioned tables of variant-trait associations with their sources, written as CSV and YAML without code. Researchers publish them to just-dna-registry, and users browse them in the web interface's Module Catalog (Figure 1a), install them and apply them to their genome, with no new software release. On 26 October 2026 the registry held 24 modules from five publishing accounts, the ten defaults of Table 1 and fourteen others (Section 3.4).
- **Fast.** On one workstation, a whole genome is annotated with the ten default modules in 33 to 65 s, 128 to 231 times faster than Generation I's configured workflow (which also runs database annotators), and a rerun on an already normalized genome takes 16 to 20 s. just-prs computes a polygenic score in a median of 0.08 s, about seven times faster than PLINK2 in our benchmark configuration, in which PLINK2 was started once per score on four threads and just-prs used sixteen (Section 4).
- **Three ways in.** The same operations are available from a web interface; from AI assistants (Claude Code, Codex, Cursor and other MCP clients) through two MCP servers and installable plugin skills; and from Python libraries and command-line tools published on PyPI (just-prs, prs-ui, just-dna-format, just-dna-compiler and the just-dna-registry client).
- **Evidence a reader can follow.** Each matched variant in a report leads to its reported effect, the module's conclusion and its sources, and each polygenic score to its PGS Catalog model, coverage and reference population (Figures 1b, 2 and 3).
- **Local.** Annotation and scoring run on the user's computer. Reference data are downloaded; the genome is not uploaded (Section 5.2).

## **3. Algorithm and Implementation: Annotation Modules and Polygenic Scoring**

Just-DNA-Lite ships with a default set of annotation modules, either curated by experts from the published literature or derived from public databases (ClinVar, Landrum et al., 2018; ClinPGx, Whirl-Carrillo et al., 2021), which illustrate the module system across several trait domains. The platform is designed so that other researchers (e.g. bioinformaticians and geneticists) can add their own modules without writing code, either by hand or with AI assistance (Section 3.4). The modules annotate user variants against published variant-trait association databases; they do not perform clinical interpretation or generate diagnoses.

The default modules originate in Generation I, the OakVar-based Just-DNA-Seq (Kulaga et al., 2024). That system was built around longevity and longevity-associated traits, which is why the default set centres on that domain; for Generation II, most modules were ported, largely unchanged, to serve as worked examples of the no-code module format rather than as a definitive trait catalogue.

The default collection brings together longevity-associated variants, disease-predisposing variants and drug-response associations (Table 1). These were the linked questions behind Generation I.

### **3.1 Longevity Variants Module (example)**

The Longevity Variants module (published as Longevity Map) connects an individual's genotypes to studies of survival and exceptional longevity, with the source populations and findings available for inspection. It builds on the LongevityMap database (Tacutu et al., 2018), which contains 3,144 variants in 884 genes (build 3, June 2017); the module keeps those with significant associations (Table S4). We did not re-annotate or re-validate these variants; our contribution is (1) curation and expansion with post-2017 literature, adding 50 new entries and editing 876 existing records; (2) assignment of weighted scores based on study quality, replication across populations and statistical significance; and (3) pathway-based categorization for structured browsing.

## **5. Discussion**

### **5.6 Conclusion**

Just-DNA-Lite gives people and researchers a local way to read a genome against published evidence. It offers a cleaned PGS Catalog of 6,117 scores with ancestry-matched percentiles and quality labels, a registry of installable annotation modules that grows as researchers publish them, whole-genome annotation in about a minute, and the same operations from a web interface, AI assistants and Python libraries. The reported benchmarks show annotation 128 to 231 times faster than the configured workflow of the OakVar-based Generation I system run on the same machine and close numerical agreement between scoring engines, on four call sets from three people for annotation and one for scoring. Evaluation across individuals, reference populations and matched-variant sets remains necessary to establish the broader reliability of these workflows. The platform provides infrastructure for research and education, without establishing clinical validity for the findings it reports. Researchers can begin with the public-data demonstration, inspect the sources behind a report, and then apply or extend the same workflow locally for their own research questions.
