\begin{abstract}
Genome reports written for clinicians are hard for lay readers to follow. We randomised 64 participants
to a layered report, which puts a plain-language summary above the technical detail, or to a standard
report. Comprehension ratings were higher with the layered report, t(62) = 3.85, p < .001, and higher
numeracy was associated with better comprehension. Layered reports therefore improve patients'
understanding of genetic risk and will reduce anxiety about genomic results in clinical practice.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Clinical genome reports are written for specialists, and lay readers find them hard to follow
\cite{haga2014,goehringer2018}. Layered formats put a short plain-language summary first and the
technical detail after it. We asked whether a layered format improves self-rated comprehension compared
with a standard report.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Participants}
We recruited 64 participants through a university volunteer register and randomised them 1:1 to the
layered or the standard report (32 per group). All gave written informed consent, and the ethics
committee of the host university approved the study.

\subsection{Measures}
Participants rated their comprehension of the report on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = did not understand,
7 = fully understood); we analysed one overall rating per participant. Numeracy was the number of items
answered correctly on an eight-item numeracy test (0--8). Confidence in explaining the result to a
relative was rated on a 5-point scale.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
We compared groups with two-sided t-tests, compared format preference with a chi-square test and related
numeracy to comprehension with Pearson's correlation. A pre-registered directional hypothesis about the
risk section was tested one-tailed. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses used
R 4.4.

\section{Results}
Table~\ref{tab:characteristics} describes the participants. Comprehension ratings were higher with the
layered report (M = 5.64, SD = 1.04, n = 32) than with the standard report (M = 4.47, SD = 1.34,
n = 32), t(62) = 3.85, p < .001. Confidence ratings also differed, t(62) = 2.10, p = .02, and a one-way
analysis of variance on the same ratings gave F(1, 62) = 4.41, p = .04.

Most participants preferred the layered format, $\chi^2(1, N = 64) = 2.71$, $p = .03$. In a one-tailed
test of the pre-registered hypothesis, risk-section ratings rose after the summary among the 31
participants who completed that section, t(30) = 1.80, p = .041. Higher numeracy caused better
comprehension, r(62) = .45, p < .001.

\section{Discussion}
In this sample of volunteers, a layered format was associated with higher self-rated comprehension than a
standard format. The volunteers were more often university-educated than the general population, and
comprehension was self-rated rather than tested, so the size of the difference may not transfer to
patients receiving clinical results. Higher numeracy went with better comprehension; this study cannot
say whether numeracy causes it.
