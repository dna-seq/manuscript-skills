# Simulated potential-user reading report

This is a simulated reader perspective, not real participant evidence. I read the complete supplied manuscript and viewed Figures 1, 2 and 3. I did not consult any other files. My assumed perspective is someone with a personal genome file who can install ordinary software and wants to explore the results, but does not routinely run bioinformatics pipelines.

**My one-sentence takeaway to a colleague:** Just-DNA-Lite lets me explore a compatible genome locally, see variant annotations linked to their source studies, and compare several polygenic scores against a reference population, although I still need to work out the installation and what results are usable for my particular file.

**What I think I would get and see.** I expect a browser interface containing my sample, a table of variants after quality filtering, a choice of annotation modules, downloadable HTML reports and a polygenic-score view. Figure 1 makes the sample and filtering part believable, although most of the visible screen is still a raw variant table rather than the information I came to learn. Section 3.1 and Figure 2 give me the clearest picture of the payoff: my called genotype could lead to a named association, a curation weight and studies from named populations. I understand that the 0.475 weight is not a number of extra years of life. For polygenic scoring, I expect several models for the same trait, their coverage and their population-relative percentiles together. I cannot treat Figure 3 as a reliable preview of the current result because its caption explicitly identifies obsolete displays.

**Whether I could run or apply it.** I could try the public demonstration without putting my own genome into it. The abstract tells me that local software is available on Windows, macOS and Linux, and Section 2.1 tells me to bring a VCF rather than raw sequencing reads. Only in Section 5.4 do I get the decisive condition that annotation and the web interface currently require GRCh38. If my file were a GRCh38 whole-genome VCF, the described workflow seems relevant to me. If I had a consumer-array download or an older-build file, I would leave unsure which of the advertised interfaces I should attempt and how much of the result would be useful. I could not start a local run from the manuscript alone: the source links give me somewhere to go, but I do not yet know the installation route, required disk space, initial download burden or likely first-run experience on my computer.

**Where my attention dropped.** These are moments in my reading, not judgments about the scientific work.

- **Opening draft notice, before the abstract:** “The boundary with the companion paper is *consuming* versus *authoring*”. I had not yet learned what I could do with my genome, but was already sorting the boundaries between two papers, several libraries and a publishing history. I skimmed the rest of that notice to find the actual description of the tool.
- **Section 2.1, Annotation Pipeline:** “built with Dagster for workflow orchestration, polars-bio for VCF reading”. The ingestion-to-report sequence helped me, but the sequence of implementation names made me wonder whether I needed to understand or install each one separately. I began looking for the next user-visible step.
- **Section 2.2, Annotation Modules as Data:** “a separate authoring route accepts `module_spec.yaml` and CSV tables such as `variants.csv` and `studies.csv`”. I understood that modules can be added, which matters to me, but stopped following the distinction between authored files, compiled artifacts and runtime tables because I was still trying to use an existing module.
- **Section 3.2, Polygenic Risk Scores:** “an SNV row scores only from a record with an SNV alternate allele”. I lost the thread inside the matching rules. I retained that the software checks alleles, but not the detailed conditions or their relation to the result I would see. The following description of memory-sized chunks added to that distance from my own workflow.
- **Section 3.2.2, Ancestry Inference:** “the online augmentation, decomposition and Procrustes method of FRAPOSA”. I could follow why a reference population is needed and that I can change it. At this phrase I no longer knew what I was meant to understand as a user, and skimmed until the rule about unknown ancestry.
- **Section 4.2, PRS Computation:** “Tables 3 and 4 retain the earlier benchmark so that its results remain traceable to the recorded protocol.” I then had to track the current version, an earlier version, two kinds of PLINK2 input and different agreement numbers. I stopped trying to retain the numbers and looked for what they implied about running my genome.

My attention returned strongly at “**A report in use.**” in Section 3.1. That is the point where I could imagine opening one of my own results and following its evidence. It returned again at the input-format and privacy limitations because those affect whether I could use the tool at all.

**Three things I remember.**

1. My genome can stay on my computer during the core analysis, but an external AI assistant can still receive sensitive results.
2. The FOXO3 example connects a G/T call to five studies, and its curation weight does not predict a change in lifespan.
3. Different polygenic models for one trait are shown together rather than reduced to one unquestioned result.

**What I wanted and did not get.** I wanted to know what the first half-hour would involve on an ordinary computer: what to install, how much to download, how much disk space to reserve and what would happen before the quoted fast analysis could start. The workstation timings do not let me picture that. I also wanted an immediate way to establish whether the file I already have is the supported kind and genome build. The late limitation gives me the rule but not how I would identify my own file.

I wanted to see a complete, current path from selecting a module to reading its finished report. The FOXO3 reconstruction answers what a result can contain, while Figure 1 stops at the variant grid. For PRS I wanted to understand what I would do when one model puts me high and another puts me near the middle, or when coverage is around half of the model. I understand that these facts are displayed; I do not yet know how I would use them to decide whether to spend time on that result. I also wanted to know what information leaves the machine when I use an AI option. The privacy discussion tells me that this can happen but does not give me a concrete example of the outgoing information.

**Terms that stopped me.** “Parquet,” “lead table,” “manifest,” “fsspec” and “artifact” made module use feel like a software-development task. “Homozygous-reference restoration” and “callability” made me pause over whether a displayed result came from my file or an assumption. “Superpopulation,” “LD proxy,” “karyotype,” “linkage pruning” and “Procrustes” required knowledge I would have to look up. I could keep reading past them, but I could not confidently explain those parts to someone else. The succession of Just-DNA-Lite, just-prs, just-prs-mcp, just-dna-format, just-dna-compiler and just-dna-registry also made it hard to remember what I personally need to install.

**What would stop me and what I would do next.** Uncertainty about my file's build and the local setup would stop me before trying my private genome. A consumer-array file would leave me particularly tentative because support is described as experimental. I would first open the public demonstration, select a public genome, find the longevity report and follow one variant to its studies. I would then look at the software's installation documentation and compare its requirements with my computer and file. If that checks out, I would try the same local workflow without an external AI service initially. After obtaining my own results, I would use the tool to explore the cited literature; this manuscript has not made me expect a diagnosis or a personal treatment decision.
