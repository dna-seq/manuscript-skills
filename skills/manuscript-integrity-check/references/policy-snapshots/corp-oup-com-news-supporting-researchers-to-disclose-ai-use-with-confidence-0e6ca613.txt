## Supporting researchers to disclose AI use with confidence

We have updated our author and editor guidelines on the responsible, appropriate, and transparent use of AI, including a policy outlining what AI use should be disclosed when preparing manuscripts for our journals.

In a recent global survey of more than 2,600 researchers, nearly three-quarters (74%) of respondents revealed they were unclear what AI use they should disclose, despite there being strong support for disclosure.

With 44% of researchers concerned that disclosing their use of AI will negatively impact how others view their work, and only 36% recording their AI use during their research, our survey emphasizes the growing gap between AI use and disclosure in academic research, and recognizes the need for clear disclosure policies.

As efforts continue to develop a global reporting standard for AI disclosure in research , our new guidelines intend to support researchers to record and disclose their AI use with confidence. The updates to our guidelines focus on:

Disclosure of AI use across both books and journal articles, with authors expected to be transparent about any significant use of AI tools

Human oversight and accountability , with authors responsible for reviewing and verifying any AI-generated content

Protection of intellectual property and confidentiality to ensure care is taken when using AI tools with unpublished work, copyrighted material, or sensitive content.

"Our research into AI disclosure follows our 2024 survey , which revealed a wide range of initial perceptions towards AI. Two years on, AI use is more widespread, but there remain major differences in how researchers are adopting, applying, and disclosing these tools.

"At OUP, we've developed AI guidelines for authors with the view that the scholarship we publish must remain valued and protected. We regularly review these against industry guidelines and consider any feedback to ensure clear guidance for authors.

## Key findings from our survey:

64% of researchers report AI as beneficial to their research, with over two-thirds (68%) using AI chatbots on the open web for their research, and just under half (49%) using machine translation.

AI tools are most commonly used for discovering existing research (55%), summarizing existing research (46%), and editing the write-up of research (45%). Many (68%) agree that AI tools can reduce accessibility barriers, too.

However, 35% of those in the arts and humanities never use AI tools in their research, as opposed to 15% and 18% for STM and the social sciences, respectively.

Only 20% trust AI-generated outputs. 37% of researchers who haven't used AI for their research report concerns about accuracy, bias, and trustworthiness, and only 26% find AI-generated literature summaries to be accurate.

"My trust in AI outputs would increase if the responses consistently included clear and verifiable sources (such as case law, treaties, or academic references), showed transparent reasoning for how conclusions are reached, and accurately distinguished between established legal authority and interpretation or opinion. It would also help if the AI acknowledged uncertainty where appropriate and avoided overgeneralization, especially in complex legal or academic topics."

However, 83% of those surveyed have established practices for checking citation sources generated by AI, and 80% always cross-check AI outputs for reliability.

Based on respondents' perceptions and behaviours towards AI, survey analysis identified five different profiles of researcher.

These range from the least trusting of AI-the 'persistent sceptics'-who are most commonly found in the arts and humanities, to the 'carefree casuals', who are less likely to record their own AI use and are more common among later career stage respondents than early career researchers.

Meanwhile, 'frustrated trailblazers' have benefited most from AI but are also the most concerned about the administrative burden of disclosing its use. They are more prevalent in the social sciences and STM disciplines, and among early career researchers.

"I am ethically opposed to AI. AI has been built on the stolen work of artists and writers. I am also concerned about the environmental consequences of AI. I grant that in some fields or in some inquiries, AI may be of some genuine use-not in my research (which is about literature). But even when it has genuine use, the ethical and environmental concerns remain. If I were to use AI on my research, it wouldn't be my research anymore."

Nearly three-quarters (74%) report uncertainty around AI disclosure requirements, while only 36% actively keep records of their AI use during research. One respondent acknowledged:

"I tend to make sure my AI use is undetectable as I'm not sure what to disclose."

44% are concerned that disclosing AI use could negatively affect perceptions of their work-many of whom are in the arts and humanities-with 35% finding AI disclosure to place an additional administrative burden on researchers.

45% of researchers report their institution's approach to AI use as unclear, while 43% report a somewhat or very enabling approach.

48% of those writing grant applications had not fully disclosed AI use, particularly in narrative drafting, translating or editing, and plain language summaries.

46% of journal authors and 39% of book authors had not fully disclosed their AI use in preparing their manuscript, particularly in narrative drafting in each case. Only a third (30%) of book authors and 42% of journal authors were asked to attest whether AI was used in their manuscript submission.

25% of peer reviewers had not fully disclosed AI use, particularly in language editing. 60% felt their AI use and disclosure complied with the reviewer policies, but the views were more mixed as to whether the journal policies on AI use were clear.

"Make the rules clear, coherent, and reasonable. Also, not all AI use should be disclosed (e.g., checking language), the same way that you do not disclose using a calculator. The question in the end is whether the author is the originator of the work in a deep sense. AI will be used."

Many authors want future AI disclosure policies to change, with some calling for clearer and more consistent guidelines on AI use and disclosure.

Others suggest policies should support a move towards the normalization of AI as a tool with minimal disclosure.

Looking at future AI disclosure policies to support researchers as readers of others' work, many want to see standardized and mandatory AI disclosure introduced.

"The policy should protect normal responsible use of AI as a tool without forcing authors into vague or excessive reporting requirements."
